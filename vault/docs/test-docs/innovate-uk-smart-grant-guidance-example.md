# Innovate UK Smart Grant — Assessor Guidance Notes (Synthetic Example)
# Note: This is a synthetic guidance document for retrieval testing only.
# Based on publicly available Innovate UK guidance principles.

---

## What Assessors Are Looking For

Innovate UK Smart Grant applications are assessed against four equally weighted criteria. Each criterion is scored 1–10 by two independent assessors. Applications scoring below 6 on any single criterion are typically rejected regardless of overall score.

---

## Criterion 1: Innovation

### Strong applications demonstrate:

- A clearly defined innovation that goes beyond incremental improvement
- Evidence of current state of the art, with specific references to academic literature, competitor products, or existing technology
- A credible explanation of why the proposed approach is technically superior
- Technology Readiness Level (TRL) clearly stated for start and end of project
- Freedom to operate analysis showing no blocking IP exists
- Patent strategy described where applicable

### Common weaknesses:

Assessors frequently penalise applications that describe the technology in detail but fail to compare it clearly with what already exists. Saying "our product is better" without evidence of what you are comparing against is insufficient. Applications should name specific competing technologies and explain the performance gap with data.

Vague TRL claims without justification are penalised. If you claim TRL 4, describe specifically what has been validated and under what conditions.

---

## Criterion 2: Market Opportunity and Commercialisation

### Strong applications demonstrate:

- A quantified total addressable market with source references
- A realistic serviceable addressable market — not the entire global market
- A specific route to market with named partners or customers where possible
- Identification of key competitors with honest assessment of competitive position
- A clear business model explaining how revenue will be generated
- Awareness of regulatory requirements and how they will be navigated

### Common weaknesses:

Applications that claim implausibly large market shares without justification. Assessors understand that a small company cannot capture 30% of a £10 billion market within 5 years of commercialisation.

Failure to identify barriers to adoption. Every technology faces inertia — assessors look for evidence the team understands why customers might not switch and has a strategy to overcome it.

Generic statements like "the market is growing rapidly" without citing sources or explaining what is driving growth.

---

## Criterion 3: Project Plan and Team

### Strong applications demonstrate:

- A realistic project timeline with clear work packages and milestones
- Milestones that are binary (achieved/not achieved) rather than vague progress measures
- A team with directly relevant expertise — not just general business or technical skills
- Named team members with specific roles and credentials relevant to this project
- An honest assessment of skills gaps and a credible plan to fill them
- A risk register that identifies technical, commercial, and management risks with specific mitigations

### Common weaknesses:

Overloaded timelines where the same person is responsible for multiple parallel work packages. Assessors calculate person-months and will flag plans that require more than 100% of available resource.

Risks that are generic ("the project might take longer than expected") rather than specific to this project. A strong risk register identifies the three or four things most likely to cause failure and explains how each is mitigated.

Milestone descriptions that cannot be objectively assessed. "Progress on membrane development" is not a milestone. "Ten membranes meeting proton conductivity >80 mS/cm as measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy" is a milestone.

## Criterion 4: Environmental Impact and Wider Benefits

### What counts as environmental impact:

Assessors assess both direct and indirect environmental benefits. Direct benefits are those arising from the product or technology itself — reduced emissions, lower energy consumption, reduced waste. Indirect benefits arise from enabling other emissions reductions — for example, better energy storage enabling more renewable energy deployment.

Both positive and negative environmental impacts must be addressed. Applications that only list benefits and ignore negative impacts score poorly. Assessors are trained to identify unacknowledged negative impacts and will penalise applications that appear to ignore them.

### Quantification guidance:

Where possible, environmental benefits should be quantified. Preferred metrics:
- CO2e avoided per unit deployed per year
- Energy saved per unit deployed per year
- Waste reduction in kg or tonnes
- Water consumption reduction in litres

Where quantification is not possible at this stage, the methodology for measuring impact should be described.

### Wider economic and social benefits:

Assessors look for benefits beyond the applicant organisation. These include:
- Direct and indirect job creation, with regional context where relevant
- Supply chain benefits to UK businesses
- Contribution to UK sector competitiveness or export potential
- Knowledge spillovers through academic partnerships or publication
- Skills development and training

Regional benefits carry additional weight for applications in Levelling Up priority areas. If your project creates jobs or develops supply chains in these areas, make this explicit.

## Common Reasons for Rejection

1. **Innovation not clearly differentiated** — The application describes what the technology does but not why it is better than existing solutions with supporting evidence.

2. **Market size not credible** — The total addressable market figure cannot be traced to a reputable source, or the claimed market share is implausible.

3. **Team lacks relevant expertise** — The team has business experience but no specific technical experience in the domain of the project.

4. **Risks not identified** — The risk register lists generic project management risks rather than technology-specific or market-specific risks.

5. **Environmental benefits not quantified** — The application claims environmental benefits without any attempt to estimate their scale.

6. **Need for public funding not justified** — The application does not explain why the project cannot proceed without grant funding, or why commercial finance is unavailable.

7. **Budget not credible** — Cost estimates appear unrealistically low or high, or staff costs are inconsistent with the proposed timeline and team composition.

## Writing Guidance

### Answer the question asked

Grant questions are specific. Read each question carefully and answer what is asked. A common failure mode is to answer a slightly different question — for example, describing the technology in response to a question about market opportunity.

### Use specific evidence

Claims should be supported by evidence. When citing market sizes, name the source. When claiming technical superiority, cite comparison data. When describing team capability, give specific relevant examples.

### Be honest about risks

Applications that present an unrealistically optimistic picture score poorly. Assessors are experienced professionals who understand that innovation is risky. Acknowledging the main technical risks and explaining your mitigation strategy demonstrates maturity and increases assessor confidence.

### Address negative impacts

If your technology has environmental or social downsides — even minor ones — address them directly. Assessors who notice an unacknowledged impact will penalise the application. Pre-empting this with a brief, honest discussion and mitigation plan turns a potential weakness into a strength.

### Keep it concise

Character limits exist for a reason. Applications that exceed limits are truncated. Applications that use 60% of available characters often score lower than those that use 95% — insufficient detail signals insufficient development of thinking.
